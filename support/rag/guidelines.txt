## Active Listening
Active listening is one of the most important skills for peer supporters.
Give the youth your full attention — put aside distractions, maintain gentle
eye contact, and use brief verbal acknowledgements ("I see", "go on") to show
you are engaged. Reflect back what you hear using their own words rather than
interpreting or judging. Avoid interrupting or rushing toward solutions.
Active listening builds trust and helps the youth feel genuinely heard.

## Safe Messaging on Mental Health
Safe messaging guidelines protect vulnerable individuals when discussing mental
health or distress. Avoid graphic descriptions of self-harm or suicide methods.
Use person-first, non-stigmatising language — say "a person experiencing
suicidal thoughts" rather than "a suicidal person". Frame help-seeking as a
sign of strength. Always provide the youth with local helpline numbers and
remind them that professional support is available.

## When to Escalate
Escalation is required whenever a youth expresses immediate risk of harm to
themselves or others, discloses ongoing abuse, or shows signs of acute
psychosis. As a peer supporter you are not a clinician — your role is to
listen, stabilise, and refer. When in doubt, escalate to the assigned
counselor through the platform. Document the reason for escalation clearly
in your session notes so the counselor has full context on arrival.

## Self-Help Tools
Self-help tools can be offered when risk is low and the youth is willing to
engage. Breathing exercises (4-7-8 technique), grounding techniques (5-4-3-2-1
senses), and brief journaling prompts are evidence-informed approaches suitable
for peer-led support. Always check in at the following session to assess
whether the tool was helpful. Self-help is a complement to, not a substitute
for, professional care.

## Confidentiality Boundaries
Confidentiality is the foundation of a trusting peer-support relationship.
Explain to the youth at the start of the relationship that what they share
stays between you and the platform — with one important exception: if their
safety or someone else's safety is at risk, information must be shared with
the supervising counselor. Never share a youth's personal disclosures with
other youth, family members, or third parties without informed consent.

## Grief and Loss Support
Grief and loss support requires patience and non-judgement. Normalise the
range of emotions — sadness, anger, guilt, relief — without prescribing how
the youth "should" feel. Avoid platitudes such as "everything happens for a
reason". Instead, acknowledge the loss directly ("That sounds like a really
painful loss") and invite the youth to share memories or feelings at their
own pace. Refer to a counselor if grief is prolonged, complicated, or
accompanied by self-harm ideation.

## Cultural Sensitivity
Cultural sensitivity is essential when working with diverse youth populations.
Be aware of how cultural background shapes the expression of distress — some
communities may use somatic complaints (headaches, fatigue) rather than
emotional language. Avoid making assumptions based on ethnicity or religion.
Ask open questions about what support looks like in the youth's family or
community, and adapt your approach accordingly. When cultural factors are
beyond your competence, consult the supervising counselor.

## Building Resilience
Building resilience in youth involves identifying and reinforcing existing
strengths rather than focusing exclusively on deficits. Ask questions like
"What has helped you get through hard times before?" Encourage connections
with trusted adults, peer networks, and community activities. Small
achievable goals build a sense of agency. Resilience is not about eliminating
stress but about developing the capacity to recover from it.

## Supporter Boundaries and Self-Care
Boundaries protect both the peer supporter and the youth. Do not share your
personal contact details or communicate outside the platform. Avoid dual
relationships — you cannot be both a peer supporter and a close friend to the
same person within the program. If you feel emotionally overwhelmed after a
session, use the platform's reflective log and seek supervision from your
assigned counselor. Burnout in helpers is real and must be taken seriously.

## Trauma-Informed Care
Trauma-informed care means recognising that many youth have experienced
adverse events that shape their behaviour and responses. Avoid re-traumatising
questions that demand detailed recounting of harmful events. Instead, focus on
safety, choice, and collaboration. Ask "What would feel helpful right now?"
rather than "Tell me exactly what happened." Assume that challenging behaviour
may be a trauma response, not defiance, and respond with curiosity rather than
frustration.

## Crisis De-Escalation
Crisis de-escalation involves staying calm, speaking slowly and clearly, and
reducing environmental stressors where possible. Validate the youth's feelings
without agreeing with harmful intentions: "I can hear how much pain you are
in right now." Avoid arguing, threatening, or raising your voice. Keep the
conversation focused on immediate safety. If the youth is in immediate danger,
contact emergency services and notify the supervising counselor immediately
through the escalation function on the platform.

## Session Structure
Session structure helps both the peer supporter and the youth feel grounded.
Begin each session with a brief check-in mood score. Agree on one or two
topics to focus on for the session. Close with a summary of what was discussed,
any agreed actions, and a reminder of when you will next connect. Structured
sessions are more effective than open-ended conversations because they give
the youth a sense of predictability and progress.
